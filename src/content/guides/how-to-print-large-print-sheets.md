When you print a phone list or a weekly schedule for an aging parent, one small setting on your computer can change how the whole page looks. Many home computers and phones shrink pages automatically without asking you first. A sheet that looked big and roomy on your laptop screen can come out of the printer with cramped boxes, narrow margins, and tiny words.

When someone is over seventy, or when you are looking for a number late at night in a dim hallway, those extra millimeters of space matter. You should not need a magnifying glass to read a phone number on the refrigerator.

Here is a calm, step-by-step routine to get full-size, easy-to-read pages from any home printer.

## 1. Always choose "Actual Size" or "100% Scale"

When you open the print window on your computer, look for a setting called **Scale**, **Page Sizing**, or **Fit**. Many home printers default to "Fit to Printable Area" or "Shrink to Fit." That setting pulls all four edges inward by about ten percent. It makes every word, checkbox, and handwriting box smaller than it was designed to be.

Switch that setting to **100%** or **Actual Size**. All of our printable sheets are laid out for standard US Letter paper (8.5 by 11 inches) with safe margins already built in around the edges. Nothing will get cut off when you print at full size.

If your parent lives in a place that uses A4 paper, you can still print the sheets comfortably. On A4 paper, choose **Fit to Printable Area** so the slightly narrower width fits cleanly on the sheet, then check the page using the arm's-length test below.

## 2. Print in plain black and white

Colored ink often looks pale under a warm kitchen lamp in the evening. Light grey lines or pastel boxes are even harder to tell apart when eyes are tired.

Before you press the print button, check the box for **Black and White** or **Grayscale**. If your printer has an "Eco," "Draft," or "Toner Save" mode, turn that mode off for these sheets. Normal black ink gives you solid dark lines and crisp headings against white paper. High contrast between dark ink and light paper is the single biggest help for older eyes.

## 3. Pick paper that feels sturdy in the hand

Standard copy paper—usually labeled **20 lb** on the wrapper—works fine if you slip the sheet inside a clear matte binder sleeve or clip it to a hard clipboard.

If you plan to tape a sheet to the refrigerator door, pin it to a corkboard, or erase and update notes over several weeks, step up to **24 lb** or **28 lb** bright white paper. You can buy a single ream at any office supply store. Heavier paper helps in three practical ways:

- It stays flat on a refrigerator door or clipboard instead of curling at the corners.
- It keeps dark pen ink from bleeding or showing through to the back of the page.
- It feels substantial and easy to pick up for hands that may be stiff in the morning.

Always choose **uncoated matte paper** rather than glossy photo or brochure paper. Glossy paper reflects overhead kitchen lights and creates glare. Matte paper stays readable from any angle.

## 4. Try the 30-second arm's-length test

Before you print a whole ten-page pack, print **one single page** first. Walk into the room where your parent will actually use that sheet. That might be right beside the kitchen landline, on the counter near the kettle, or on a side table next to their favorite armchair.

Stand under the normal room light and hold the printed page out at full arm's length. Ask yourself three simple questions:

- Can I read the main headings and box labels right away without squinting?
- Are the blank writing spaces tall enough for unhurried, everyday handwriting?
- Do the lines look dark and steady against the paper?

If your parent is home with you, hand them the test page and a pen. Notice whether they have to pull the sheet close to their face or tilt it toward a window. If they can read the labels comfortably at arm's length and write a name inside a box without crowding the lines, your printer settings are spot on.

## 5. Keep the right pen right beside the page

Even the clearest worksheet is frustrating if the only pen nearby is a dried-out ballpoint that needs hard pressure. Ballpoint pens often leave thin, faint lines that are tough for older eyes to read back the next day.

Put a medium-point black or dark blue rollerball pen, or a fine felt-tip marker (around 0.7 mm to 1.0 mm tip), right next to the clipboard. Those pens glide across the paper with almost no hand pressure and leave a bold, dark line. Tie one pen to the clipboard with a piece of string or keep two pens in a heavy mug right beside the binder so nobody ever has to go hunting through drawers.
